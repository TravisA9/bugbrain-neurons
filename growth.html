1 · The idea

Nobody wires these brains by hand and nothing is trained by backpropagation. A brain is grown: a few seed cells in a flat sheet of tissue divide, choose what to become, send out axons and make synapses. Every cell follows the same rules (one genome) and only uses what it can sense locally: chemicals, its own genes, the cells its growth cone touches.

The grown network is then a population of spiking neurons (leaky integrate-and-fire, 4 substeps per tick). During its lifetime it learns with local rules: a synapse only uses the activity of its two cells plus a broadcast signal (dopamine or a teacher input). Evolution can change the genome; the hand-made body plans are genomes written by us as starting points.

2 · One genome in every cell gene regulatory network

Each cell carries an expression state of 10 genes. Every developmental step it updates them from what it senses: s = tanh(Wg · [7 local chemicals, s, developmental time, 1]). A second matrix turns the genes into 46 cell decisions, o = sigmoid(R · [s, 1]): divide, die, migrate, emit a signal, become a neuron and grow an axon, be inhibitory, threshold, slow (memory) membrane, plastic output, receptive to plastic input, address and seek tags, guidance preferences, axon shape (collaterals, bifurcation, projection), topographic label, claw or mixing dendrites.

Same genome, different place → different chemicals → different genes on → different cell types. The matrices Wg and R are the genome (plus some cell-scale numbers such as the tissue size or the synaptic budgets).

Biology: every cell has the same DNA; cell types arise from transcription-factor networks reading positional signals.

3 · Morphogens: where am I? sources and gradients

Seven chemicals give each position an address. Fixed sources: midline, anterior (front edge), sensor cue (emitted by the environment's sensors), motor cue (emitted by the output cells) and a central zone stripe. Two more, signal A and B, are emitted by cells themselves (as much as their genes say) and diffuse and decay on the grid; their decay lengths are genes.

Layers come from thresholds on a gradient: in the prediction plans, staircase genes switch on sharply where the anterior signal crosses a set value, so the sheet is cut into bands, one cell type per band (memory M, previous-symbol memory M2, resets, conjunctive C, mixing G, prediction P).

Biology: morphogen gradients (e.g. Bicoid, Shh) and threshold-reading genes ("French flag") pattern embryos into stripes and layers.

4 · Cells divide, choose a fate, die

  • Division: a proliferation gene driven by developmental time ("clock") lets seed cells divide while it is on; daughters land next to the mother (up to a cell budget).
  • Fate: once a cell becomes a neuron it fixes its type: excitatory or inhibitory, threshold, membrane leak (slow cells hold memory), plastic or not.
  • Death: a DIE gene removes cells where they are not wanted (e.g. the top band). After wiring, receptive cells that got no sensory (instructive) input die (afferent-dependent survival), and neurons starved of trophic factor die (section 7).

Biology: proliferation windows, fate commitment, programmed cell death; in the vertebrate nervous system about half of the neurons made die again.

5 · Axons find their partners tags and guidance

A neuron's growth cone climbs (or descends) the chemical mix its genes chose: positive guidance weights attract, negative repel. Each cell shows an address tag T ("who I am", 8 numbers) and its axon carries a seek tag S. When the cone touches a neuron, a synapse forms with strength ∝ S_pre · T_post; no match, no synapse.

  • Midline rule: after an axon crosses the midline its midline attraction turns into repulsion, so it does not cross back. Commissural axons make no synapses before crossing.
  • Collaterals: up to 4 short local side branches (gene COLL, length CLEN).
  • Bifurcation: the axon rises, then splits into two branches running left and right through a whole layer, synapsing all the way (parallel fibres); a delayed split happens only where a signal is high enough.
  • Projection axons synapse only on outputs. Axons that found nothing may keep growing (growth until satisfied).

Biology: netrin / robo (midline crossing, robo3 for commissural axons), ephrin / Eph and cell-adhesion codes (partner matching), cerebellar granule cells (T-shaped parallel fibres).

6 · Topographic columns and claws

Cells with the TOPO gene keep a positional label (their x position when they became neurons, or, with input instruction, the label of the nearest input channel). Synapses between labelled cells are scaled by exp(−(Δlabel / topoW)²), so the input for symbol i, the memory cells under it and output i form a column, for any number of symbols. The learnable (plastic) projections ignore labels: they cross all columns.

Claw cells (conjunctive layer C) receive their own column's input plus parallel fibres from all previous-symbol columns, but keep the fibres of only one randomly chosen column. With a fast membrane and a small input budget they fire only for one pair (previous j, current i). Mixing cells (G) keep the inputs of only a few random C cells (4) of their column: sparse codes for combinations.

Own-column reset (v19, plan predictK2XO): inhibitory claw cells (the X2 cells that erase the previous-symbol memory M2 at the start of each slot) keep their own column instead of a balanced pick. Each column's reset cell is then driven by its own symbol and silences exactly its own memory column; before, about half of the 24 memory columns of a text brain were never loaded or never cleared. Text accuracy rose from 0.71 to 0.76.

Biology: retinotopic / barrel maps (ephrin gradients, thalamic instruction); cerebellar granule-cell claws with 4–5 mossy-fibre inputs; Kenyon cells of the insect mushroom body with a few random inputs each.

7 · Hunger and trophic signals

  • Neurotrophic competition: outputs release a fixed amount of trophic factor. It is shared among their inputs by synaptic weight and passed back up the chain (hidden targets add a little of their own). Neurons that receive too little die: no useful output, no survival.
  • Hunger (gene, used by plan v3h): an output with too few inputs, or a sensor with too few targets, builds up a "need". Needy outputs emit an attractant that pulls growing excitatory axons, and tag matching is relaxed in proportion to the need, so no output stays orphaned.
  • Request (optional): spontaneous sensor activity during growth marks the cells it reaches; a little later the paired outputs call for them.
  • Connection demand (v27, default in predictK3 and rewardE): an output with fewer than 3 excitatory inputs releases an attractant in proportion to how many it is still missing. It spreads and fades with distance, so nearby growing excitatory axons turn towards it. It only attracts: tag matching is not relaxed, so an axon still needs the right partner tag. A sensor still short of targets, or an axon that has found no partner while some output is still in demand, grows one stretch further. The signal switches off by itself once the output is satisfied. Demand is kept apart from the other signals: spontaneous activity during growth (request), prediction errors (teacher plateau), reward (dopamine) and activity balance (homeostasis). In R28, demand raised output coverage with 6 bandit arms from 72% to 94% and left no orphan outputs. No task got worse.

Biology: neurotrophins (NGF, BDNF) from targets keep innervating neurons alive; target-derived attractants (netrin-like chemoattraction by uninnervated targets, which stops once innervation is complete).

8 · Tuning the grown circuit

  • Synaptic scaling: each neuron scales its excitatory (and inhibitory) inputs from hidden cells to a fixed total (genes normE / normI), so its drive does not depend on how many axons happened to find it. Claw and mixing cells have their own budgets (clawNorm, clawIn, mixNorm).
  • Juvenile phase: spontaneous waves sweep across the sensors. Homeostatic thresholds: each neuron moves its threshold (and scales its inputs) toward a target rate. Hebbian refinement: inputs that reliably make the cell fire are strengthened, rarely co-active ones are pruned.
  • Random dendrite selection after growth for claw and mixing cells (section 6).

Biology: synaptic scaling (Turrigiano), retinal waves before eye opening, activity-dependent pruning ("fire together, wire together").

9 · Lifetime learning after growth, local rules only

One rule (v22, the default plans): every plastic synapse changes by dw = lr · M · e. Here e is what the synapse recently did (its eligibility), and M is a third factor that tells the cell whether that was good. Which signal M is depends on the target cell:

  • A cell contacted by a topographic sensory input gets a local instructive signal, like a cerebellar Purkinje cell with its climbing fibre. When the right answer arrives as strong input to that cell, it enters a short plateau, and that plateau is M. It acts on the fresh eligibility: inputs active just before are strengthened.
  • Every other plastic cell gets the global dopamine reward prediction error (r − V from a critic), like a striatal neuron. It acts on a slowly fading eligibility trace, so it can reach the choice that earned the reward.

The same genes and the same parameter set give teacher-like learning on text and reward learning on the bandit and the cue task. It matches the separate rules within noise: text 0.773 / 0.769, order3 0.970 / 0.968, bandit 0.653 / 0.653, cue 0.855 / 0.855. When the dopamine was also applied to taught cells, text learning collapsed: the instructive signal is the better teacher where one exists.

Mechanisms that shape the learning (older plans use them separately):

  • Reward-modulated STDP (bug arena): every plastic synapse keeps an eligibility trace of pre-before-post timing; a global dopamine signal (food +, poison / wall −) turns it into a weight change.
  • Teacher-gated plasticity (sequence prediction): the next symbol arrives as input to its own prediction column, putting those cells into a short plateau. Plastic fibres from cells that were active a moment earlier (a lagged trace) onto plateau cells are strengthened: "what was active before b comes, predicts b". Each cell's plastic outputs are normalised to a fixed total, so they become probabilities of what follows.
  • Metaplasticity (metaN): a cell's learning step shrinks with the potentiation it already received, so its weights become running counts.
  • Learned trust (trustPow): each cell keeps a running score of how much of its output weight lands on the cell that is then taught, and scales its output budget by it: reliable, specific cells outvote vague ones.
  • Divisive normalisation (rcvDiv): the plastic input to the prediction layer is divided by its total, so the relatively strongest prediction fires even when all are weak (gain control).
  • Adaptive mixing cells: feedforward inhibition (mixInh) from the cells that drive the mixing layer keeps it sparse, and each mixing cell has an intrinsic homeostatic threshold (mixHomeo) that rises after each spike and slowly falls, so it fires rarely but not never. (A competitive Hebbian rule for their inputs, mixLr, exists but did not help.)
  • Optional: post-teacher afterhyperpolarisation (teachAHP): when a column's teacher input ends it is briefly inhibited, so it cannot echo the current symbol into the prediction window (off: it removes the small early echo but does not raise accuracy).

Biology: dopamine-gated STDP (basal ganglia); climbing-fibre teaching signals and plateau potentials (cerebellum, hippocampus); metaplasticity (BCM); divisive normalisation (cortex); APL / Golgi feedforward inhibition and intrinsic plasticity of Kenyon / granule cells (mushroom body, cerebellar granular layer); the pause after a Purkinje complex spike.

10 · A context cascade shift register across regions (v21)

To predict the next letter, the brain must know what came before. The text plan (predictK2XO) holds the current symbol (M) and the previous one (M2). M2 is filled by a clock: before each symbol, GO2 clears M2, then GO1 copies M into M2 and clears M. predictK3 extends this into a cascade. A second region, grown by the same gene program, keeps only its front end. Its memory columns receive the cortex's M2 through a topographic tract. A third clock phase, GO3, clears them one tick before GO2, so they hold the symbol two steps back (M3). M3 then projects back to the cortex's mixing cells. Each mixing cell keeps one column of M3 along with its M and M2 inputs, so it responds to a three-symbol context.

Result: on a third-order pattern the brain goes past the two-symbol limit (0.75 → 0.97). Cutting the M3 tract brings it back to exactly the two-symbol level. Text improves a little (0.763 → 0.772). There are many more three-symbol contexts than mixing cells, so coverage is the limit.

Biology: delay lines and sequential memory buffers (prefrontal persistent activity, hippocampal time cells, songbird HVC chains); clock-gated transfer is like theta-phase-locked updating of working memory.

11 · Regions and tracts a brain of several tissues (v21)

A region genome lists regions. Each one has its own gene program, its own inputs and a colour. Each region grows on its own tissue, with its own morphogen gradients, bands, cell types and juvenile activity. This is like the territories that early patterning marks out in the neural tube (forebrain / midbrain / hindbrain), where each territory then develops on its own. Tracts are then laid between regions:

  • topo: column to column, preserving the map, like a thalamocortical projection.
  • claw: each mixing cell takes one source column, like mossy fibres onto granule cells.
  • all: plastic parallel fibres onto every receiving cell, like corticostriatal fibres.

Axon pathfinding between tissues is not simulated cell by cell. The tract rule names the populations, and the map says how axons pick targets. The result is one network that keeps the env's inputs and outputs. One region carries the outputs and the plasticity genes. In the viewer, a region selector shows each tissue. The wiring diagram rings every population in its region's colour.

v30: one genome for every task. The default for text and three-phase sequences, the bandit and random-mapping cue/recall is now unifiedR: a single genome with three areas (cortex, context, striatum). Each area carries receptor genes for two morphogen-like signals read from the task: a multi-phase clock (sequence tasks) and a dopamine/outcome source (reward tasks). Areas whose expression stays low do not develop; so sequence tasks grow cortex + context (= predictK3) and reward tasks grow the striatum (= rewardE). Same brains, same scores (16 held-out growths per task); the difference is that one genome now decides.

v31: a better reward area. The striatum of unifiedR now grows from rewardE2, re-evolved with two eligibility tags per synapse (a fast one, about 8 steps, and a slow one, about 25 steps, mixed 24:76) and receiver homeostasis. 16 held-out growths: bandit and random cue unchanged (6.70 / 10.10 vs 6.69 / 10.17), symbol-to-arm pairings learned from reward much better (seqchoice cycle 6.40 vs 4.55).

v32: tasks with both signals. When a task brings a dopamine/outcome source, the striatum (rewardE2) now always develops fully and owns the outputs; if it also brings a phase clock, the cortex and context areas develop too, smaller (graded), and feed the striatum through their mixing cells. On seqchoice this beats the striatum alone (cycle 6.78 vs 6.40, order2 5.20 vs 4.52); text, order3, bandit and cue grow exactly the same brains as before.

v33: an evolved reward area. The striatum of unifiedR now comes from a brain evolved on bandits with 3 and 6 arms and the symbol-to-arm tasks (rewardE3). Only that area was taken over: the whole evolved brain had lost its sequence skills. Bandit with 6 arms 5.11 vs 4.31; other tasks unchanged within noise; text and order3 grow the same brains as before.

v34: a deeper memory for sequences. The text and sequence default is now deepURSx3: unifiedR plus a chain of three context areas. Each context area is driven by the one before it, so the chain holds progressively older context; all of them feed the cortex mixing cells. The chain is repressed by the outcome signal, so reward tasks grow as before.

v35 to v37: tuning the teacher rule for long lives. v36 removed the prediction-miss weakening in the cortex and capped its metaplastic consolidation (metaCap 200), so learning keeps a floor rate instead of fading out over a long life; the readout became graded (a small trained read-in, readIn 0.01). v37 doubled the cortex learning rate (0.05 to 0.1) and gave the striatum its prediction-miss weakening back (predErr 0.3), which seqchoice needs.

v38: a faster engine. The same model and rules computed more cheaply (shared-factor traces, teacher updates only for active cells, a leaner membrane loop). It is not bit-identical (rounding differs), so it was checked by scores on 16 growths per task: no task worse, 2 to 3 times faster.

v39: a faster cortex rate. The cortex learns at 0.2 (was 0.1): about +0.006 to +0.009 on book text and +0.007 on fables, order3 and seqchoice unchanged (16 fresh growths). Higher rates (0.3, 0.5) gain more on book but start to forget order3.

v40: a larger presynaptic budget in the cortex. Each cell projecting onto the cortex keeps a fixed total of outgoing plastic weight; v40 raises it from 45 to 55. Small but clean: on 16 fresh growths no task was worse (order3 +0.005 on 8 of 8 changed growths, book and fables +0.001 to +0.002). Larger budgets (65, 80) trade fables for book. In a 4× cortex the larger budget costs order3, so scaled runs keep 45.

Honest status (October 2026): deepURSx3 is the default for text, sequences and seqchoice. On real book text it predicts the next letter about 33% of the time on held-out text; a readout trained directly on its own activity reaches about 40%, so the learning rule leaves room. Bigger brains (4× the cells, with axon reach and growth speed scaled to the bigger sheet) gain about 2 points on book and fables but lose a lot on order3 and the reward sequence task, so size is not part of the default. Longer lives (100,000 letters instead of 30,000) add almost nothing at any size yet. The brain generates babble with some real words, not sentences.

12b · Growing where learning fails experimental, off by default (R29)

A test of adult neurogenesis. The learning layer keeps a slow average of its own errors: missed predictions for prediction cells, and negative reward-prediction errors for reward cells. When that average stays high, quiet reserve cells mature. Each one takes inputs from the sources that were active just before the errors, sends weak plastic synapses to the layer's targets, and is pruned again if those synapses don't strengthen. A metabolic cost and a cap limit growth. In half-size brains it recovers about a quarter of what text and fables lose, but it doesn't reach the full grown brain, so it stays off.
